Building Reading Comprehension: A Parent's Guide

Updated September 2026 · 10 minute read

📋 At a Glance

  • Who this is for: Parents of children roughly ages 5–10 who can decode words but need stronger understanding of what they read
  • Time needed: A few minutes of conversation before, during, and after any reading session — no extra time block required
  • Materials: Any book your child is already reading
  • Skills covered: Predicting, questioning, summarizing, visualizing, vocabulary in context

A child can read every word on a page correctly and still walk away having understood almost nothing. That gap surprises a lot of parents, because reading aloud smoothly looks like understanding. Comprehension is a separate skill from decoding, and — like decoding — it can be taught directly, not just absorbed automatically through practice.

1. The "Simple View" of Reading Comprehension

One of the most useful, well-established frameworks in reading research is the simple view of reading: reading comprehension is the product of two separate skills — decoding (turning print into words) and language comprehension (understanding what those words mean when strung together, the same skill used in understanding spoken conversation).

If either skill is weak, overall comprehension suffers, even if the other skill is strong. A child with excellent decoding but weak vocabulary and background knowledge will still struggle to understand what they read — and a child with rich spoken language skills but shaky decoding will also struggle, simply because too much effort goes into sounding out words.

2. Why Fluent Reading Doesn't Always Mean Understanding

Reading specialists sometimes call this "word calling" — a child reads the words accurately and even with decent expression, but couldn't tell you what just happened in the story. This is common at a specific stage: decoding has become smooth enough to sound effortless, but it still quietly uses up most of a young reader's mental effort, leaving little left over to think about meaning.

The fix generally isn't more decoding practice — it's building automatic decoding (so it truly requires no conscious effort) alongside direct comprehension practice, rather than assuming comprehension will simply show up on its own once decoding is fluent.

3. Four Strategies That Actually Build Comprehension

These four strategies, used before, during, and after reading, are among the most consistently supported by reading research — far more effective than comprehension worksheets alone.

StrategyWhen to Use ItExample Question
PredictingBefore reading, and at natural pause points"What do you think will happen next?"
QuestioningDuring reading"Why do you think she did that?"
VisualizingDuring reading"Can you picture what that room looks like?"
SummarizingAfter reading (or after a chapter)"Tell me what happened, in just a few sentences."

💡 Parent Tip

You don't need to use all four strategies every time — even one or two thoughtful questions woven naturally into a bedtime story does more for comprehension than a stack of worksheets.

4. What to Expect by Age

AgeTypical Comprehension Skill
4–6Retells simple stories in order; answers basic "who" and "what" questions about a story read aloud
6–8Makes simple predictions; explains why a character did something; summarizes a short story in a few sentences
8–10Makes inferences beyond what's directly stated; compares characters or events; identifies the main idea of a longer passage
10+Reads critically — evaluates an author's argument, recognizes bias, connects ideas across a whole book

5. Activities for Building Comprehension

🔮 Prediction Pause

Stop at a natural cliffhanger moment and ask what your child thinks happens next — then keep reading to check. There's no wrong answer; the thinking is the point, not accuracy.

🎬 Retell It Like a Movie Trailer

After finishing a book or chapter, have your child summarize it in one exciting sentence, like a movie trailer voiceover. Keeps summarizing playful instead of feeling like a test.

❓ Question Swap

After reading, take turns asking each other questions about the story. Kids often ask more thoughtful questions when quizzing a parent than when being quizzed themselves.

6. Common Struggles and What's Really Going On

"My child can answer questions but can't summarize on their own."

Summarizing is one of the hardest comprehension skills, since it requires deciding what matters most and leaving the rest out. Start by summarizing together out loud before expecting an independent written summary.

"My child understands stories but not nonfiction."

Nonfiction comprehension relies heavily on background knowledge and unfamiliar text structures (headings, captions, diagrams) that stories don't use. Reading nonfiction together and explicitly pointing out those features helps more than assuming the skill transfers automatically from story reading.

"My child seems to understand out loud but does poorly on written comprehension questions."

This is often a writing or working-memory bottleneck, not a comprehension problem — holding an idea in mind while also forming it into written sentences is its own separate skill. Letting your child answer out loud first, then write, can help isolate where the real difficulty is.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Why can my child read a book aloud perfectly but not tell me what happened?

This is often called "word calling" — decoding is smooth enough to sound fluent, but so much effort goes into the words that little is left for meaning. It usually improves as decoding becomes fully automatic and comprehension strategies are practiced directly.

What is the "simple view of reading"?

A well-established framework stating that reading comprehension is the product of decoding skill and language comprehension. A weakness in either one limits overall comprehension, which is why both need attention.

Does reading comprehension improve just from reading more?

Reading volume helps, but comprehension improves faster when paired with active strategies like predicting, questioning, and summarizing, rather than silent reading alone.

Should I stop and explain every word my child doesn't know?

Not every time — that breaks the flow of the story. Explain words essential to the plot, and let your child guess at less important ones from context.
