Dyslexia-Friendly Activities: A Practical Guide for Parents

Updated September 2026 · 11 minute read

What this guide is: General information about dyslexia and widely-used, evidence-informed teaching strategies that help many dyslexic learners and struggling readers more broadly. What it isn't: A diagnostic tool or a substitute for a formal evaluation and professional reading intervention. Only a qualified professional can diagnose dyslexia; this guide is meant to help you understand the pattern and support your child while pursuing that evaluation.

📋 At a Glance

  • Who this is for: Parents who suspect dyslexia, have a diagnosed child, or have a reader who isn't responding to typical phonics instruction
  • Core idea: Multisensory, highly structured, explicit phonics instruction — not more of the same approach, repeated harder
  • Materials: Sand tray or textured surface for tracing, letter tiles or cards, audiobooks alongside print books

Dyslexia is one of the most common and most misunderstood learning differences. A lot of the stigma comes from a myth worth dismantling immediately: dyslexia has nothing to do with intelligence. It's a specific difference in how the brain processes the sounds and symbols of written language, and it occurs across every level of ability. With the right kind of instruction — which is different from typical instruction, not just more of it — dyslexic readers learn to read well.

1. What Dyslexia Actually Is

Dyslexia is a specific, well-researched learning difference that primarily affects the connection between written letters and their sounds, making decoding and spelling harder than expected given a child's overall intelligence and effort. It's not related to how hard a child is trying, how smart they are, or how much they've been read to. Many dyslexic children have strong vocabularies and rich understanding when material is read aloud to them — the gap shows up specifically in independent decoding.

2. Common Signs Parents Notice

These are commonly reported patterns, not a diagnostic checklist. A single item on this list, especially in a young child, is not unusual — persistent difficulty across several of these, despite good instruction and time, is what generally prompts a formal evaluation.

  • Persistent difficulty connecting letters to sounds despite consistent, good-quality phonics instruction
  • Reading that stays slow, effortful, and halting well past the typical age for that stage
  • Letter reversals (b/d, p/q) that persist noticeably longer than the typical range
  • A clear gap between strong understanding when listening and much weaker performance reading the same material independently
  • Difficulty with rhyming or breaking words into sounds, even with practice
  • A family history of similar reading difficulties (dyslexia has a genetic component)

3. Core Principles for Dyslexia-Friendly Teaching

CumulativeNew skills build directly on mastered ones, with regular review built in
OverlearningMore repetition than feels necessary, until a skill is truly automatic, not just "learned once"

4. Strategies That Help

✋ Multisensory Letter-Sound Teaching

Say the sound, trace the letter in sand or on a textured surface, and build the word with physical letter tiles — engaging sight, sound, and touch together rather than looking at a page alone. This approach (often associated with Orton-Gillingham-based programs) is widely regarded as one of the most effective for dyslexic learners specifically.

🎧 Audiobooks Alongside Print

Listening to a book while following along in print (or listening separately for content access) lets a child engage with age-appropriate stories and information without every experience of books being a struggle. This is a bridge to build vocabulary and comprehension, not a replacement for decoding instruction.

⏱️ Extra Time and Reduced Reading Load

Where possible, reduce how much independent reading is expected in a single sitting, and allow extra time for reading tasks. This isn't "letting a child off the hook" — decoding genuinely takes more cognitive effort for a dyslexic reader, and rushing undermines both accuracy and confidence.

5. Protecting Confidence Along the Way

Dyslexic children often work harder for the same result as their peers, day after day, which puts them at real risk for reading-related anxiety and low self-esteem if it isn't addressed directly. Celebrate effort and specific progress, not just correct answers, and make sure your child has regular access to books and subjects they can enjoy without the added weight of decoding — through audiobooks, being read to, or high-interest topics matched to a comfortable reading level.

💡 Parent Tip

Explicitly separate "how smart you are" from "how reading feels right now" in conversations with your child. Many dyslexic adults report that hearing this distinction clearly, and early, made a real difference in how they saw themselves.

6. What to Avoid

🚫 Common Pitfalls

  • Relying on whole-word visual memorization — this specifically tends not to work well for dyslexic readers and can mask the underlying decoding gap temporarily, only for it to resurface with new, unmemorized words
  • Assuming low effort or attention is the cause — dyslexia is a processing difference, not a motivation problem
  • Adding pressure or timed drills before the foundation is solid — speed should come after accuracy and confidence, not before
  • Waiting to see if it resolves on its own — persistent, specific reading difficulty benefits from earlier evaluation, not a wait-and-see approach

7. Working With Schools

Many school systems offer formal support plans for identified learning differences, along with accommodations like extra time or access to audiobooks. Ask your child's school specifically what evaluation and support options exist locally — this varies by school system and region, so it's worth asking directly rather than assuming.

8. When to Seek a Formal Evaluation

⚠️ Don't Wait Too Long

Unlike some developmental differences that often resolve with time alone, persistent, specific difficulty connecting letters to sounds — especially alongside a family history or a clear verbal-versus-reading gap — tends to benefit from earlier evaluation and intervention. If this pattern persists despite good instruction, raise it with your child's teacher or pediatrician sooner rather than later. Earlier support generally leads to better outcomes.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Is dyslexia related to intelligence?

No. Dyslexia is a specific difference in how the brain processes written language, and it occurs across the full range of intelligence.

What are common early signs of dyslexia?

Persistent difficulty connecting letters to sounds despite good instruction, slow effortful reading well past the typical age, persistent letter reversals, and a gap between strong listening comprehension and weaker independent reading. These are patterns to discuss with a professional, not a self-diagnosis checklist.

What is multisensory reading instruction?

An approach that teaches letter-sound connections through sight, sound, and touch together, rather than visual memorization alone — often associated with Orton-Gillingham-based programs and well-regarded for dyslexic learners.

Should I wait to see if my child grows out of a reading difficulty before seeking evaluation?

Generally, no. Persistent, specific reading difficulty consistent with dyslexia tends to benefit from earlier evaluation and intervention rather than a wait-and-see approach.
