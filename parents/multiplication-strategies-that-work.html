Multiplication Strategies That Actually Work: A Parent's Guide

Updated September 2026 · 10 minute read

📋 At a Glance

  • Who this is for: Parents of children roughly ages 7–10 learning or practicing multiplication facts
  • Time needed: 5–10 focused minutes a day, mixing new facts with review
  • Materials: Paper, dice or small objects for grouping, nothing expensive
  • Skills covered: Conceptual understanding, skip counting, times table patterns, fact fluency

Multiplication is often the first math skill that asks a child to do two different things at once: truly understand a new concept, and memorize a large set of facts well enough to recall them instantly. Doing both together is what makes it feel harder than addition or subtraction did. The good news is that both parts have real, well-tested strategies — this isn't a subject where "just practice more" is the whole answer.

1. Why Multiplication Trips Kids Up

Addition and subtraction can largely be figured out by counting. Multiplication tables, though, ask for near-instant recall of 100+ separate facts (1×1 through 10×10) — counting your way to every answer is technically possible but far too slow to be useful in later math. That combination of "understand a new kind of grouping" plus "memorize a lot, fast" is what makes this stage feel like a jump in difficulty, even for kids who did fine with earlier math.

2. The Right Order: Understanding Before Memorizing

It's tempting to go straight to flashcards, but starting with the concept pays off. A child who understands that 4 × 3 means "4 groups of 3 things" can:

  • Reconstruct a forgotten fact by counting groups, instead of being stuck
  • Apply multiplication to real word problems, not just bare number facts
  • Understand why the times tables are symmetrical (4×3 = 3×4)

A useful general approach in math education moves from concrete (physical objects, like grouping actual counters) to pictorial (drawings of groups or arrays) to abstract (the bare number sentence, 4 × 3 = 12). Skipping straight to the abstract stage before the concept is solid is a common reason facts don't stick well.

3. Strategies for Each Times Table

Times TableStrategyWhy It Works
×2DoublingSame as adding the number to itself — usually the easiest table
×4Double, then double again4 = 2 × 2, so ×4 is just doubling twice
×8Double three times8 = 2 × 2 × 2, builds directly on the ×2 and ×4 facts
×5Skip count by 5s; notice the pattern (ends in 0 or 5)Very predictable pattern, often the second table mastered after ×2
×10Add a zeroSimplest pattern in the whole table
×9The "finger trick" or (×10, then subtract the original number)9× anything = 10× that number minus the number itself
×3, ×6, ×7Skip counting and fact familiesThese don't have a single simple trick — most benefit from steady spaced practice

💡 Parent Tip

Teach the ×2, ×5, and ×10 tables first — they're the most pattern-friendly and give a child several "easy wins" before tackling the trickier ×3, ×6, ×7, and ×8 facts.

4. A Realistic Memorization Routine

Short and frequent beats long and rare. A workable weekly rhythm:

DayFocus
Mon–WedIntroduce or review one times table (e.g. ×3), using grouping/arrays first, then bare facts
ThursdayMixed review — quiz facts from all tables learned so far, not just the newest one
FridayA quick, low-pressure game (see activities below) instead of a formal drill

Mixing a few new facts with regular review of older ones (sometimes called "spaced practice") produces far better long-term recall than drilling one table in isolation until it's "done" and never revisiting it.

5. Hands-On Activities

🍬 Array Snack Time

Arrange crackers or small snacks into rows and columns (like 3 rows of 4). Count the total together, then write the matching multiplication fact. Eating the "manipulatives" afterward is a bonus most kids enjoy.

🎲 Dice Multiplication War

Each player rolls two dice and multiplies the numbers. Whoever has the higher product keeps both dice. A fast, competitive way to get repeated low-stakes practice.

🏀 Skip Counting Bounce

Bounce a ball while counting by a chosen number (2s, 5s, 10s) out loud. The physical rhythm helps the counting pattern stick in memory.

6. Common Struggles and Fixes

"My child mixes up multiplication and addition."

This usually means the concept of "groups of" hasn't fully clicked yet. Go back to physical grouping — actual objects arranged into equal groups — before returning to number sentences.

"My child knows the facts but is very slow to answer."

This is a normal in-between stage. Many kids first solve facts by skip counting (correct, but slow) before shifting to instant recall. Continued light, frequent practice — not pressure to go faster — tends to close this gap naturally over time.

"My child has memorized facts but can't apply them to word problems."

This is a sign the concept, not just the facts, needs more attention. Go back to concrete grouping and picture-drawing for word problems specifically, even if bare number facts are already solid.

⚠️ When It Might Be More Than Typical Difficulty

Most multiplication struggles resolve with the right kind of practice over a few months. It's worth mentioning to a teacher if your child has persistent, significant difficulty across many areas of math (not just times tables), especially if it's paired with difficulty judging quantities or sequencing numbers generally. This is general guidance, not a diagnosis — a teacher can help determine if further evaluation makes sense.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

At what age do kids usually learn multiplication?

Most children are introduced to multiplication concepts around age 7-8, with full times table fluency typically expected by age 9-10. This varies by curriculum and individual child.

Should my child memorize times tables or understand multiplication first?

Understanding should come first. A child who understands the grouping concept can reconstruct a forgotten fact and apply it to word problems, unlike pure memorization alone.

What is the fastest way to memorize times tables?

Short, frequent sessions mixing a few new facts with review of old ones, paired with pattern-based tricks rather than pure rote repetition.

My child can skip count but still can't answer multiplication facts quickly. Is that normal?

Yes, this is a common intermediate stage. With continued practice, most children gradually shift from counting to automatic recall.
