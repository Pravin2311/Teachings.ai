Four is a big year — most children move from recognizing a handful of letters to genuine early reading and math readiness, while also becoming much more capable, independent playmates. This hub pulls together what's typical at this age, and points to the specific free activities and guides on Teachings.ai that match where a 4-year-old actually is.

1. Developmental Snapshot

Every child develops at their own pace — these are general patterns, not a checklist to hit on schedule.

Motor skillsCan use scissors with some control, draw basic shapes, hop on one foot briefly
Social-emotionalPlays cooperatively with other children, begins to take turns, expresses emotions more clearly in words

6. Social & Emotional Learning

Four-year-olds are getting much better at cooperative play, sharing, and naming their feelings — but still need help managing big emotions. Simple, consistent language ("I can see you're frustrated — let's take a breath") helps far more than long explanations at this age.

7. Motor Skills

  • Fine motor: Playdough, stringing large beads, using child-safe scissors, tracing shapes
  • Gross motor: Hopping, catching a large soft ball, riding a tricycle, simple obstacle courses

8. Screen-Free Activity Ideas

💡 Try These

  • A letter or color scavenger hunt around the house
  • Building block towers and counting how many blocks tall
  • Sorting toys or laundry by color or size
  • A simple obstacle course in the backyard or living room

10. Frequently Asked Questions

What should a 4-year-old know academically?

Most 4-year-olds recognize many letters (especially in their name), count a small set of objects accurately, know basic shapes and colors, and are starting to connect letters to sounds. This varies widely between children.

How much structured learning time does a 4-year-old need?

Roughly 15-30 minutes total a day of adult-led, focused activity is generally plenty, spread across short bursts rather than one long session.

Is my 4-year-old behind if they don't know all their letters yet?

Not necessarily. Most children recognize most or all uppercase letters somewhere between ages 4 and 5, so a 4-year-old still learning letters is generally within the normal range.
