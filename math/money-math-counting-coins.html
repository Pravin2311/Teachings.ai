💰 Money Math: Counting Coins and Making Change

Updated October 2026 · 10 minute read

📋 At a Glance

  • Starting age: Basic coin ID around 5-6, mixed counting and change around 7-8
  • Why coins are tricky: Value doesn't match size — a dime is smaller than a nickel but worth more
  • Best change-making strategy: Counting up from the price, not subtracting

Money math is one of the most naturally motivating areas of math practice — real or play coins make the abstract concept of value concrete, and the skill has obvious real-world payoff.

1. Coin Identification

CoinValue
Penny1 cent
Nickel5 cents
Dime10 cents
Quarter25 cents

Start by sorting coins into piles by type before introducing values — recognizing the coins by appearance is a separate first step from knowing what they're worth.

2. Counting Mixed Coins

Unlike counting identical objects one by one, coins have different values regardless of size — a dime is smaller than a nickel but worth more, which genuinely confuses many kids at first. Counting mixed coins requires skip-counting by different amounts depending on which coin comes next (starting with the largest value coins first tends to be easier): count quarters by 25s, then dimes by 10s, then nickels by 5s, then pennies by 1s.

✅ Sort Largest to Smallest Before Counting

Arranging mixed coins from highest to lowest value before counting reduces the mental juggling — counting quarters first, then dimes, then nickels, then pennies is far more manageable than counting in random order.

3. Making Change by Counting Up

Counting up from the price to the amount paid, rather than subtracting, tends to be more intuitive for kids. If something costs $3 and payment is $5, count up from $3 to $5 using coins or bills ("$3... $4 is one more dollar... $5 is another dollar... that's $2 in change") rather than calculating $5 minus $3 abstractly.

4. Real-World Practice Opportunities

A pretend store using household items with price tags, or genuine small purchases at an actual store with adult supervision, both provide real practice that a worksheet alone can't replicate — the stakes of a real (even small) transaction tend to make the skill stick faster than abstract practice.

5. Frequently Asked Questions

What age should kids start learning to count coins?

Basic identification around age 5-6, with mixed-coin counting and making change typically following around age 7-8.

Why is counting mixed coins harder than counting objects?

Coins have different values regardless of size, requiring skip-counting by different amounts depending on which coin comes next.

What's a simple strategy for teaching how to make change?

Counting up from the price to the amount paid, rather than subtracting, tends to be more intuitive.
