Reading Comprehension Questions to Ask After Any Book

Updated October 2026 · 10 minute read

📋 At a Glance

  • Works with: Any book — these questions aren't tied to a specific title or curriculum
  • Best practice: Two or three genuine questions, not a long checklist
  • Four thinking levels: Recall, inference, connection, and evaluation

The questions below work with picture books and chapter books alike, and don't require having read the book yourself in advance. Picking one or two per conversation, rather than working through the full list, keeps it feeling like a real conversation about the story.

1. Why Question Levels Matter

Not all comprehension questions ask for the same kind of thinking. Some just check whether a child was paying attention to what happened; others ask them to think beyond the page. Mixing question levels — rather than sticking only to easy recall questions — builds a fuller kind of comprehension over time.

2. Recall Questions

Answers are stated directly in the text — these check basic attention and memory:

  • "What did the main character want at the start of the story?"
  • "Where did this part of the story take place?"
  • "What happened right before [a specific event]?"

3. Inference Questions

Answers require reading between the lines — these build deeper comprehension:

  • "Why do you think the character made that choice, even though the book didn't say exactly why?"
  • "How do you think the character felt in that moment? What makes you think that?"
  • "What do you think might happen next, based on what you know so far?"

✅ "What Makes You Think That?" Is the Key Follow-Up

Asking a child to explain their reasoning after an inference question is often more valuable than the initial answer itself — it reveals whether they're genuinely reading between the lines or just guessing.

4. Connection Questions

These link the book to the child's own life or other stories:

  • "Has anything like this ever happened to you?"
  • "Does this remind you of another book or movie? How is it similar or different?"
  • "Would you have made the same choice as the character? Why or why not?"

5. Evaluation Questions

These ask for a judgment or opinion, backed by reasoning — usually the hardest level for younger children:

  • "What was the most important moment in the story? Why?"
  • "Did you like how the story ended? What would you have changed?"
  • "Was the main character a good friend/leader/problem-solver? What makes you say that?"

6. How to Use These Without It Feeling Like a Quiz

Two or three genuine questions, asked in the flow of an actual conversation, work far better than moving methodically through a checklist. Sharing a reaction first ("I was really surprised when...") before asking a question often invites a more honest response than opening with a direct question. If a child seems resistant right after finishing a chapter, waiting for a more natural pause later in the day — in the car, at a meal — usually works better than a formal after-reading discussion.

🚫 Worth Avoiding

  • Asking every question on this list in one sitting — this turns a conversation into an interrogation
  • Only asking recall questions — easy to do since they're quick to think up, but they build the shallowest kind of comprehension on their own
  • Correcting an inference as "wrong" when it's simply different — as long as the reasoning is grounded in the text, a different interpretation is a sign of genuine thinking, not a mistake

7. Frequently Asked Questions

How many questions should I ask after reading a book?

Two or three genuine questions usually work better than a long checklist — the goal is a real conversation, not a quiz.

What's the difference between recall and inference questions?

Recall answers are stated directly in the text. Inference questions require reading between the lines. Both matter, but inference builds deeper comprehension.

What if my child doesn't want to answer any questions after reading?

Asking during a natural pause later, or sharing your own reaction first, often gets a more genuine response than a formal after-reading quiz.
