1. Consonant Digraphs

Two letters combining to make one new sound, different from either letter alone:

DigraphSoundExample Words
sh/sh/ship, fish, shell
ch/ch/chip, lunch, chair
th/th/ (voiced or unvoiced)this, thin, bath
wh/w/ (or /hw/ in some accents)whale, when, wheel
ph/f/phone, dolphin, graph

2. Consonant Blends

Two or more letters where each individual sound is still heard, just blended quickly together:

BlendExample Words
bl, cl, fl, gl, pl, slblack, clap, flag, glad, plan, slip
br, cr, dr, fr, gr, pr, trbrush, crab, drum, frog, grape, prize, trip
sm, sn, sp, st, swsmile, snail, spin, stop, swim

✅ Quick Digraph vs. Blend Check

If you can still hear both individual sounds when saying the word slowly, it's a blend (b-l-ack). If the two letters make one totally new sound, it's a digraph (sh doesn't sound like "s" plus "h").

3. Silent E (Magic E)

A silent e at the end of a word often makes the preceding vowel say its long name instead of its short sound:

Short Vowel WordWith Silent E
capcape
kitkite
hophope
cutcute

4. Vowel Teams

Two vowels together that combine to make one sound, often (but not always) following the "when two vowels go walking, the first one does the talking" pattern:

Vowel TeamSoundExample Words
ai / aylong arain, play
ee / ealong etree, eat
oa / owlong oboat, snow
oovaries (moon vs. book)moon, book

5. R-Controlled Vowels

When a vowel is followed by "r," the r changes the vowel's usual sound in a fairly predictable way:

PatternExample Words
arcar, star, farm
er / ir / urher, bird, fur (all similar sound)
orfor, corn, storm

6. Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between a digraph and a blend?

A digraph makes one new sound from two letters (sh). A blend keeps both sounds audible, just said quickly together (bl).

What is the silent e rule?

A silent e at a word's end often makes the preceding vowel say its long name — "cap" becomes "cape." Like most phonics rules, it has exceptions.

What does "r-controlled vowel" mean?

When a vowel is followed by r, the r changes the vowel's usual sound in a predictable way, as in car, her, bird, for, and fur.
