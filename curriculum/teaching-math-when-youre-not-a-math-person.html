Teaching Math When You're Not a Math Person

Math is one of the most common sources of anxiety for homeschooling parents — and one of the most fixable. The subject rewards a specific, learnable approach more than it rewards natural talent, which is good news for anyone who doesn't consider themselves "a math person."

1. Why Parent Math Anxiety Matters

Children are often more attuned to a parent's tension around a subject than the parent realizes, even when nothing is said out loud. A sigh before opening the math book, or a rushed "let's just get through this," can quietly signal that math is something to dread — separate from anything about the content itself. The fix isn't pretending to love math; it's being honest about the difficulty while still modeling a willingness to work through it.

✅ Narrate the Struggle Instead of Hiding It

"This one's tricky, let's figure it out together" models real problem-solving far better than either faking confidence or apologizing for not knowing the answer immediately.

2. Rusty Memory Isn't the Same as Can't Teach It

Not remembering how to do long division doesn't mean an inability to teach it — it means the material needs a quick refresh first. A good curriculum explains the reasoning behind each step, not just the mechanical procedure, which means a parent can relearn a concept alongside the lesson rather than needing to already have it memorized.

3. Practical Strategies

4. Using Outside Resources Without Guilt

Once content moves well past what staying one lesson ahead can comfortably cover — often somewhere around upper-level algebra or beyond — bringing in a tutor, a co-op class, or a structured video course is a completely reasonable choice. It's not a sign that homeschooling "failed" at math; it's the same decision a school makes when it hires a specialist teacher rather than expecting every teacher to cover every subject alone.

🎓 Outside Help Is a Tool, Not a Last Resort

Bringing in outside math instruction earlier, by choice, is just as valid as waiting until it feels unavoidable — there's no requirement to struggle through it solo as long as possible before asking for help.

5. Building Confidence Over Time

Confidence with teaching math tends to build the same way a student's confidence does — through repeated, low-stakes practice. Each year of staying one step ahead builds a stronger base for the next, and many parents find that math anxiety fades noticeably by the second or third year of homeschooling, simply from repetition.

6. Common Mistakes

🚫 Worth Avoiding

  • Skipping the "why" and only teaching the steps — this often just delays confusion rather than preventing it
  • Avoiding math anxiety by avoiding math — inconsistent math instruction usually creates more stress later, not less
  • Comparing your teaching to a "natural math person" — a structured, patient approach often produces stronger results than raw comfort with the subject
  • Waiting too long to get outside help when it's genuinely needed, out of a sense that asking means failing

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to be good at math to teach it to my child?

No — staying one lesson ahead and using a curriculum that explains the reasoning covers most of what's needed at the elementary and middle school level.

Does a parent's math anxiety affect their child?

It can — children often pick up on tension around a subject. Naming the discomfort out loud tends to model healthier problem-solving than hiding it.

When should I bring in outside help for math?

Once content moves past what a lesson-ahead approach can cover comfortably, a tutor, co-op class, or video course is a reasonable and common choice.
