👩‍🏫 Teacher Instructions

In the Classroom

Before independent work, solve one addition and one subtraction problem together on the board, thinking out loud ("I have 8, I need 3 more, so I count up: 9, 10, 11"). Encourage students who find a problem hard to use counters or draw simple tally marks rather than guessing — the goal at this stage is accurate strategy use, not pure mental math speed, which comes later with practice.

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent Instructions

At Home

If your child gets stuck, physical objects (buttons, dry pasta, coins) make the problem concrete again — this isn't a step backward, it's exactly how the concept was likely first taught in class. Point out the connection between addition and subtraction when it comes up ("if 5 + 3 = 8, then 8 - 3 must be 5") since noticing that relationship is often more valuable than memorizing each fact in isolation. ✅ Answer Key & Self-Checking Strategy

How to Check Without a Printed Key

Since the specific problems vary across worksheet printings, here's a reliable way to check any addition or subtraction answer without needing a separate key:

  • For addition: The answer should always be larger than both numbers being added (if not, something's off).
  • For subtraction: Add the answer back to the number being subtracted — it should equal the original number (e.g., if 9 - 4 = 5, check that 5 + 4 = 9).
  • Quick estimate check: Round each number to the nearest 5 or 10 first and do the easier version in your head — if the worksheet answer is wildly different from that estimate, double-check the work.

This "check by reversing the operation" habit is worth teaching directly — it's the same self-checking skill used in much more advanced math later on.
